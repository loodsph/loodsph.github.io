## Memory is not a Hard Drive

The first mistake we make is thinking of the brain as a computer or a video camera. We think that memories are files stored in a drawer, ready to be retrieved intact.

The reality is much more fascinating: memory is **reconstructive**. Every time we remember something, we are not "playing back" the past, we are rebuilding it in the present. In this process, the brain inserts pieces, modifies details, and adapts the memory to what it knows today.

## [Experiment](https://www.jstor.org/stable/20182936)

A group of people, who had visited Disneyland as children, were shown some advertising material. In these ads, a smiling Bugs Bunny could clearly be seen welcoming visitors to the magical world of Disney.

After looking at this material, the participants were asked: "Do you remember meeting Bugs Bunny during your visit to Disneyland?"

A significant percentage of the participants (in some studies up to 30-40%) not only answered "Yes", but provided incredibly vivid details:
"Yes, I remember he shook my hand."
"I remember he hugged me."
"I remember touching his long ears."

They were emotional, clear, real memories.

There is only one small detail: **Bugs Bunny is not a Disney character**.

Bugs Bunny is the symbol of Warner Bros (the Looney Tunes). He is Mickey Mouse's historical rival. He has never been, and probably never will be, at Disneyland.

The brain receives an input: "Amusement park" + "Cartoons". It accesses your general knowledge: Bugs Bunny is a famous cartoon.
When the idea is suggested (via the fake advertisement), the brain "pastes" Bugs Bunny into the Disneyland scenario to please the narrative.

We are unwitting victims. Our brain is genuinely creating a false memory based on logical (for it) associations, but historically incorrect ones.

This is why I find all the narrative about the "good old days" quite naive. The nostalgia for a time that, in fact, was only lived vicariously. A distillation, inevitably "false," of other people's memories. The critique of "today's youth" that has been passed down from generation to generation since the times of Socrates.

> A memory is not true just because it is vivid.
> Memory serves to give us a coherent sense of identity, not to provide us with documentary evidence for a trial.
