These days I hear a lot of talk about gold and commodities. *"They've hit record highs."* When the topic of money comes up, I have two certainties:

1. The time for messing around is over (the age when you start getting serious has arrived).
2. Here comes the phrase straight away: *"We need to invest in gold because it keeps going up."*

*"But does it make sense to invest in gold?"*

Given that I am not a financial advisor, that giving financial advice without being one is a crime, and that I particularly care about my money and my personal freedom: I never answer precisely. I never tell anyone what they should do. But I can say what I think, and above all, I can show data. Which is what we do here.

### FOMO (Fear Of Missing Out)

FOMO is that cognitive BIAS that affects us all. It gives you that sense of urgency of having to do something because you're missing a train that won't come back. That thing that makes mattress sellers say "UNREPEATABLE OFFER". And it's almost never true. The sliding doors over which we have real decision-making power are, imho, very few, and the purchase of a single rising asset is not one of them. It would be like reading about someone winning the lottery and starting to gamble.

### The wrong question

First, a small premise:
How are we doing in terms of financial literacy in Italy?
Bad, very bad. In the [2023 OECD global report](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2023/12/oecd-infe-2023-international-survey-of-adult-financial-literacy_8ce94e2c/56003a32-en.pdf), Italy ranked dead last out of 26 countries examined, well below the OECD average and far below the **global** average.
Among younger people, things [aren't any better](https://www.bancaditalia.it/statistiche/tematiche/indagini-famiglie-imprese/alfabetizzazione-giovani/index.html).

"Investing in gold" makes no sense as an isolated phrase. No asset makes sense on its own. Every financial instrument has a specific purpose and a specific percentage within a portfolio, based on the goals you have set for yourself.

This is the most interesting part of personal finance and the one nobody ever pays attention to, I'm sorry to say. It forces you to reckon with yourself, to put your priorities in black and white.

Gold, for example, is historically a safe haven: when stock markets do poorly, gold tends to rise. This makes it useful not as an investment to get rich, but as a counterbalance. If you have everything in stocks and the market smells a crisis (and sooner or later the crisis comes), the gold component rises, keeping the overall value of the portfolio balanced. Unfortunately, the reverse is also true: generally, when the market rises, the price of gold falls. In recent years, the correlation is no longer so clear, proving how predicting the market is more of a fortuneteller's job than an analyst's.

It's a bit like owning an umbrella company and a sunglasses company. If it rains, the umbrellas do well and the sunglasses do poorly. If it's sunny, vice versa. You always ensure a profit — sure, less than if you had invested everything in umbrellas during a year of eternal rain. But it's impossible to predict the weather for the next ten years. Just as it is impossible to predict the market.

> (For the more ~~pain-in-the-ass~~ pedantic readers: *"But that's not true! There are funds that beat the market blabla"* — ok, except for extremely rare exceptions like the late Jim Simons' Medallion Fund, which we mere mortals do not have access to).

So already in itself, it makes no sense to invest everything in a single asset. And while we're at it, a quick note: there are still people who physically buy gold. It's 2026, we've already been to the Moon, and there are funds (ETCs) that track the price of gold and buy it for you at very low costs, without the risk of someone breaking into your house and tearing your safe out of the wall.

Another thing to consider is that gold is a speculative asset by nature: unlike a stock, its price is not anchored to profits or cash flows. Investing in an index means investing in companies that produce goods and services. Gold doesn't generate profits, it doesn't pay dividends, it doesn't produce anything — it has value because we decided it has value. This is one of the reasons that pushes investors, even very experienced ones, to keep gold out of their portfolios. I see it differently: I don't hold it because I believe it will go up, I hold it because when everything else goes down, historically it goes up. It's my insurance policy, not my investment. Gold has an important track record as a counterbalance and I think it's right to keep it in the portfolio, knowing what it is and what it is not.

### The real problem: numbers are impressive

Until I really got my hands on the mathematics and mechanics of finance, confusion reigned supreme. I would see a chart with spiking prices and be impressed. Objectively, amid all those charts and big words — economists tend to unnecessarily overcomplicate simple things, I believe it's a matter of gatekeeping — I felt lost and inadequate.

*(The more attentive readers will notice a recurring theme if they have read the article on the real estate market).*

The point is that looking at a nominal price and saying *"it's at historical highs!"* is like looking at a car's speedometer and saying it's going very fast without knowing if it's on the highway or in a parking lot. The context is missing. And the context, in finance, is called **inflation**.

### What inflation is (seriously)

Inflation is that mechanism whereby what once bought you a night out at the pizzeria for ten thousand lire, today with five euros won't even cover the cover charge.

It is, substantially and perhaps fortunately, an inescapable thing. Complaining about inflation is a bit like complaining about gravity: you can do it, but it changes nothing.

The Italian CPI (the consumer price index calculated by ISTAT) measures exactly this. From 1970 to today, Italy has accumulated over 2,700% inflation. What cost 1,000 lire in 1970 would cost about 28,000 today.

So saying "the price of gold is going up" makes no sense.
I remember once, as a child, I asked my father: "Dad, is 1 second a lot or a little?"
"Well it depends: in Formula 1 it's a lot, in the Giro d'Italia not much (but it still makes a difference), in everyday life it's nothing."
The same applies to markets.
You have to ask yourself: is it rising more or less than inflation? Because if gold goes up 5% but inflation is at 6%, in real terms you've lost purchasing power.

### The data at hand

> **A quick premise:** For simplicity, let's treat the S&P 500 as shorthand for "global stock market". It's not true, it's the index of the 500 best American companies. The correct index would be the FTSE All-World, but clean historical data is a pain in the ~~ass~~ neck to implement in the script. The approximation is nevertheless methodologically correct for the reasoning.

The nominal value of gold (solid line) is already set by default in the script. Now turn on real gold (dashed) and see how deceiving it can be to only consider the sticker value. Activate the logarithmic scale if the numbers get too compressed.

### Some interesting takeaways:

1. **Gold at its 1980 peak:** Nominally it cost about 1,130 €/oz. In real 2026 terms? Over 10,000 €/oz. Anyone who bought gold in 1980 thinking they made the deal of a lifetime had to wait more than forty years to see it return to those real levels. The double-digit Italian inflation of the '70s and '80s (peaking at 21.1% in 1980) made that price monstrously inflated in terms of purchasing power.
2. **Silver is even more brutal:** The speculative peak of the Hunt brothers in 1980 (almost 38 €/oz nominally, over 300 €/oz real) has never even been approached. If you had bought silver in 1980, in real terms you would still be operating at a loss. After 46 years.
3. **The S&P 500 wins over the long run:** Despite terrifying stock market crashes (2000, 2008, 2020), the American stock market — in real terms, adjusted for Italian inflation — has beaten both gold and silver from 1970 to today. Be careful though: this calculation assumes the reinvestment of dividends. Companies generate profits that you reinvest, creating compound interest. Gold is an inert metal; it produces nothing. Not reinvesting dividends is the first fatal error of a stock investor.

### So what?

The point is that there is no magic bullet. There is no single thing where *"I do this and I'm sorted"*. To invest sensibly you need to:

* **Critically analyze** yourself and your goals.
* **Diversify** (umbrellas and sunglasses).
* **Study a bit**, which in 2026 mainly means learning methods and concepts and being able to select appropriate sources.

When someone tells you that gold is at all-time highs, ask: *"Nominal or real?"*
If they look at you confused, you already know everything you need to know about the quality of the advice.
